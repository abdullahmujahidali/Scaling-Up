Health checks: how the load balancer knows a server is alive

"Running" and "actually able to serve" are two different things. A tiny /health endpoint tells them apart.

~8 min One concept: health checks Win: keep dead servers out of rotation

30-second warm-up (recall the heartbeat from failover)

From memory: what does the heartbeat do in active–passive failover?
It's a small "still alive?" ping between two servers every second or so. When the spare stops hearing it, it assumes the active died and takes over. A health check is the same idea — but the load balancer sends the ping, and it asks not just "alive" but "able to serve."

If you remember one thing Each server exposes a tiny endpoint (like /health) that returns 200 OK when it's fine. The load balancer pings it regularly; if it fails a few times, the server is pulled out of rotation (no traffic) until it's healthy again. That's how a dead server stops silently swallowing requests.

The story: the nurse's rounds

Picture a hospital ward. Every little while a nurse does rounds — walks up to each patient and checks their pulse. Most patients respond: pulse fine, next one. But one patient doesn't respond. The nurse doesn't leave them sitting in the ward — she moves them to intensive care (out of the normal rotation) until they're stable. They come back onto the list only when the pulse is good again.

The nurse = the load balancer. The pulse check = the health check ping. The patient who doesn't respond = a server failing /health. Intensive care = pulling it out of rotation. Simple, and that's the whole idea.

Liveness vs readiness — an important distinction

"Healthy" means two different things, and keeping them apart matters both in interviews and in real outages:

  • Liveness — is it running at all? The process is alive and not hung. If liveness fails, the right fix is to restart the process. Keep it cheap — no DB query.
  • Readiness — is it ready to take traffic? Startup finished, DB reachable, cache warmed. If readiness fails, the process is fine but just don't send traffic yet — pull it from rotation, don't restart it.

A new server still booting is alive but not yet ready. A server whose DB connection dropped is alive but not ready. This distinction saves you from a "restart loop" — because if you treat readiness as liveness, the orchestrator keeps killing a perfectly fine process.

Django bridge In Django, make a lightweight /health/ view that just returns HttpResponse("ok", status=200) — no auth, no ORM query, no template render. That's your liveness check: cheap and instant. Then a separate /ready/ view that's slightly deeper — e.g. run connection.cursor().execute("SELECT 1") to check Postgres is reachable. In Kubernetes, the livenessProbe hits /health/ (fail → restart the pod) and the readinessProbe hits /ready/ (fail → remove pod from the Service, stop traffic). Careful: if your /health checks the DB, Redis and Celery on every request, one slow DB can get all your healthy servers marked "unhealthy" at once — and a small hiccup topples the whole fleet. That's exactly why /health should need no auth and do no heavy work.
Quick check: a freshly deployed server still running migrations — does it pass liveness or fail readiness?
Both apply! The process is alive (liveness passes), but it's not ready to take traffic yet (readiness fails). The load balancer should keep it out of rotation until startup finishes — and should not restart it, or it will never get ready.

The tradeoff — name it in interviews The depth of a health check is a tradeoff. Too deep (check DB + Redis + downstream APIs on every ping): it creates its own load, and one downstream hiccup marks the whole fleet unhealthy at once — a cascade failure. Too shallow (just "the process is alive"): you miss real failures — the server keeps saying "OK" while its DB is dead and it only serves 500s. The sweet spot: liveness cheap, readiness just deep enough to catch real un-readiness — and even then with timeouts and circuit-breaker thinking.

Your win today Health checks: each server exposes a tiny /health endpoint; the load balancer/orchestrator pings it regularly and pulls the server out of rotation on failure. Keep liveness (running? → restart on fail) separate from readiness (ready for traffic? → remove from rotation on fail). Keep /health cheap and auth-free, and understand the depth tradeoff — too deep cascades, too shallow misses real failures.
