Stacks & queues: which end you add and remove from

Two simple ways to add and remove items — LIFO vs FIFO. A pile of plates, a line at a shop. That's it.

~7 min One concept: LIFO vs FIFO Win: pick the right one for your access pattern

30-second warm-up (recall arrays vs linked lists)

From memory: why is adding at the end of a Python list cheap, but adding/removing at the front expensive?
A list is a contiguous array. Appending at the end is ≈ O(1). But inserting/removing at the front means shifting all the other elements over by one — O(n). Hold that thought: it comes back in today's queue choice.

If you remember one thing Stack = LIFO (Last In, First Out) — add and remove from the same end, like a pile of plates. Queue = FIFO (First In, First Out) — add at the back, remove from the front, like a line at a shop. Your access pattern decides which one.

The story: a pile of plates and a shop line

Keep two everyday pictures in mind. First: a pile of plates. You stack clean plates one on top of another. When you need a plate, you take the one on top — the one you put down last. Last in, first out. That's a stack (LIFO). Second: a line of people at a shop. Whoever came first stands at the front and is served first; new people join at the back. First in, first out. That's a queue (FIFO).

There's only one difference between them: which end you remove an item from. A stack removes from the same end it adds to. A queue removes from the opposite end. The whole concept rests on that one line.

Where you actually meet them

  • Stack: the call stack of function calls (recursion — the last call made returns first), undo/redo in a text editor (last action undone first), and the browser's Back button (the last page you visited comes back first).
  • Queue: a task/message queue processing jobs in order (whatever was enqueued first runs first), and a printer queue (documents print in the order sent).
Quick check: why is undo/redo a stack, not a queue?
Because you always want to undo the most recent action first — exactly like a pile of plates. If undo reverted your first action (FIFO), it would undo the wrong thing. Undo needs LIFO, so it's a stack.

Python bridge You can already build both in Python. Stack: a plain list — append() to push, pop() to remove. Both work at the end, both ≈ O(1). Queue: here's the easy trap. People use a list and pop the front with list.pop(0) — but that's the warm-up problem: front pop is O(n) (everything shifts). Use collections.deque instead: append() at the back, popleft() from the front — both O(1). It's the same arrays-vs-linked-lists tradeoff, guided by your access pattern.
The tradeoff — name it in interviews Match the structure to your access pattern. Choosing the wrong one is a classic performance bug: list.pop(0) inside a loop is O(n) per call, so the whole loop becomes O(n²) — fine on a small list, it blows up on production data. Want a queue, reach for deque; want a stack, a list is fine. Choosing deliberately is a senior signal.

Your win today Stacks and queues: LIFO (a pile — add/remove at the same end, like undo and the call stack) vs FIFO (a line — add at the back, remove from the front, like message and printer queues). In Python: a list for a stack, collections.deque for a queue (O(1) front pop, no list.pop(0) O(n) trap). Matching the structure to the access pattern — that's the win.
