If you remember one thing "Nines" is just how we score uptime. 99.9% = "three nines." 99.99% = "four nines." Each extra nine means ten times less allowed downtime — and usually a lot more money.

The story: a coffee shop's opening hours

Imagine a coffee shop that promises to be open. "Available" sounds nice but means nothing until you ask: open how much of the time? We measure that as a percentage of the year.

The catch is that the numbers look almost identical but mean wildly different things. "99.9% open" and "99.99% open" are one tiny digit apart — but one lets the shop close for almost 9 hours a year and the other only 52 minutes. That's a 10× difference hiding in one decimal place.

AvailabilitySpoken asClosed per yearClosed per day
99%two nines~3.65 days~14m 24s
99.9%three nines~8h 46m~1m 26s
99.99%four nines~52m 36s~8.6s

So when someone at work says "we need four nines," they're really saying "we can only be down about 9 seconds a day." That's a strong promise — and each nine you add gets dramatically harder and pricier to keep.

The part that surprises people: combining services

Here's where the math flips your gut. When you connect services together, the total availability is not the same as each piece. It depends on how they're connected — in a chain, or as backups.

In a chain (sequence): availability goes DOWN

Story: to buy a coffee you must pass through three doors in a row — each door is open 99.9% of the time. You only succeed if all three happen to be open at once. Every extra door is one more thing that can be shut. So the total is lower than any single door.

Multiply them: 99.9% × 99.9% × 99.9% ≈ 99.7%

In parallel (backups): availability goes UP

Story: now there are three side-by-side doors, each 99.9% open, and you only need one of them open to get in. For you to be locked out, all three must be shut at the same moment — which is very rare. So the total is higher than any single door.

Two 99.9% doors in parallel ≈ 99.9999% (six nines!)

Why this matters at work & in interviews Your Django API probably calls an auth service, then a payments service, then a database — all in a chain. Even if each is "99.9%," your real availability is lower (~99.7%) because they multiply. Saying that out loud — "these are in sequence, so our effective availability is below any single one" — is a genuine senior signal. And the fix is redundancy: put backups in parallel to pull the number back up.
