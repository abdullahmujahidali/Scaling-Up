The prompt: "Design a URL shortener" (like bit.ly)

Turn example.com/some/very/long/link into short.ly/aB3x9, and when someone visits the short link, redirect them to the long one. Let's run the 5-step framework.

Step 1 · Scope — what & how big

Your turn first — what questions would you ask?

Reveal my scoping
Features (in scope): create a short link; redirect a short link to the long URL. Out of scope: user accounts, analytics dashboards, custom aliases (cut them to stay focused).
Scale questions: how many new links/day, how many redirects/day? Key insight: this is massively read-heavy — few creates, tons of redirects. That one fact drives the whole design.

Step 2 · Estimate — rough numbers

Reveal the napkin math
Say 100M new links/day (writes) but 10B redirects/day (reads) — a 100:1 read:write ratio. Reads dominate. That immediately says: we'll need heavy caching and read replicas (Week 1!). Storage: 100M/day × a few years ≈ billions of rows — big, but each row is tiny.

Step 3 · Draw — the high-level design

Reveal the boxes & arrows
DNS → load balancer → app servers (stateless!) → check cache (Redis) → on miss, hit the database. That's the whole happy path — and you drew every box in Weeks 1–2:
• Load balancer + stateless servers → Lesson 3

Step 4 · Deep-dive — the two hard parts

(a) How do we generate the short code?

Reveal
Give each link a unique number (an ID) and encode it in base62 (a–z, A–Z, 0–9) to get a short string like aB3x9. 62⁷ ≈ 3.5 trillion combos in 7 characters — plenty. The database's primary key gives us the unique number for free.
click to reveal

(b) How is the redirect so fast at 10B/day?

Reveal
The lookup is "short code → long URL." Two moves make it instant:
1. Index the short-code column (B2) so the DB jumps straight to the row, no full scan.
2. Cache hot links in Redis (Lesson 4) — most redirects are for a small set of popular links, so they never even touch the database. And add read replicas (Lesson 7) for the rest.
click to reveal

Step 5 · Trade off — what did we give up?

Reveal the tradeoffs to name out loud
• Consistency: a brand-new link might take a moment to appear on all read replicas (eventual consistency, 2a) — totally fine here; nobody notices a 1-second delay before a link works.
• CAP: we'd lean AP (Lesson 5) — always redirect, even if slightly stale, over refusing.
• SQL vs NoSQL: simple key→value lookups at huge scale (Lesson 6) actually suit a key-value/NoSQL store well — a defensible choice worth stating.
