Arrays vs linked lists: seat number or treasure hunt

Two basic ways to store a sequence — and why Python's list and deque exist for different jobs.

~8 min One concept: array vs linked list Win: pick the right one by cost

30-second warm-up (recall Big-O)

From memory: what do O(1) and O(n) mean — one line each?
O(1) — constant time: no matter how big the data, the work stays the same (an instant jump). O(n) — grows directly with the data: 500 items means up to 500 steps in the worst case. Today we'll see why the same operation can be O(1) in one structure and O(n) in the other.

If you remember one thing Array = fast jump, slow middle edit. Linked list = slow jump, fast edit. An array's items sit in one continuous block (jump straight to item #500), but inserting/deleting in the middle means shifting everything. A linked list is scattered, each item points to the next (cheap to splice anywhere) — but to reach #500 you must walk from the start.

The story: numbered cinema seats vs a treasure hunt

An array is like a cinema row of numbered seats, all packed together. Someone says "go to seat 500" and you walk straight there — no counting, because the seats are in order and in one block. That's the O(1) jump. But now insert a new seat in the middle — at seat 250? Everyone after it must shuffle down one seat. That's the O(n) shift.

A linked list is like a treasure hunt. Each clue tells you where the next clue is. Splicing in a new clue is easy — just change two slips' pointers, nobody else moves. That's the O(1) edit. But to reach clue 500 you must follow clue 1 to 2, 2 to 3… all of them. No shortcut. That's the O(n) walk.

The cost table (this is the real difference)

OperationArrayLinked list
Get item #500 (by index)O(1) — instant jumpO(n) — walk from start
Insert/delete in the middleO(n) — shift the restO(1) — if you're already there
Append at the endO(1) amortizedO(1)
Insert at the frontO(n) — shift everythingO(1)

Note that "if you're already there" — a linked list's edit is only O(1) when you're already standing at the spot. Getting to the spot can still cost O(n).

Django/Python bridge Python's list is actually a dynamic array — not a linked list. That's why my_list[500] is instant (O(1) index) and my_list.append(x) is fast. But my_list.insert(0, x) or my_list.pop(0) — at the front — is O(n), because every item shifts over. When you need fast add/remove at both ends (like a queue or sliding window), Python gives you collections.deque — backed by a linked-list-like structure, so appendleft() and popleft() are O(1). That's why people reach for deque in queue-style work or BFS, not a plain list.
Quick check: you have a 100k-item Python list and keep calling list.pop(0). Why is that slow, and what should you use?
list is an array, so removing from the front shifts all the remaining 99,999 items left one slot — each pop is O(n). Use collections.deque: its popleft() is O(1) because the front pointer just moves, nothing shifts.

The tradeoff — name it in interviews Arrays = fast random access, slow middle edits. Linked lists = fast edits, slow access. Neither is "better" — it depends on your access pattern. Lots of index-by-position? Array. Lots of inserting/removing at the ends and no need to jump randomly? Linked list (or deque in Python). The right answer is: "depends on whether I jump around or edit the ends more."
You need to repeatedly read items by position from a million-item sequence (like item #742, then #10, then #900). Which structure is better?
You're building a queue: always add at one end and remove from the other — over and over. What's best in Python?
Your win today You can now pick between two ways to store a sequence by cost. Array (Python list) — one continuous block, O(1) jump by index, but O(n) middle/front edits. Linked list (like Python's deque) — scattered nodes, O(1) edits at the ends, but O(n) access by position. Before your next data-structure choice, ask: "do I jump more or edit more?" — and reach for the right one.
