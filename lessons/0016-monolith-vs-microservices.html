Monolith vs microservices: one big app or many small ones

The architecture debate everyone has an opinion on. Here's the actual tradeoff, minus the hype.

~9 min One concept: this architectural split Win: argue either side honestly

30-second warm-up (recall transactions)

From memory: what does a transaction guarantee, and what does a lock prevent?
A transaction makes multiple steps all-or-nothing (survives crashes). A lock stops two concurrent operations from corrupting the same row (survives race conditions).

If you remember one thing A monolith is one app that does everything. Microservices split it into many small apps, each owning one job, talking over the network. Microservices buy independence — and pay with complexity.

The story: one big restaurant vs a food court

Monolith = one big restaurant. One kitchen, one menu, one team. Simple to run: everything's in one place, easy to coordinate. But if the kitchen catches fire, the whole restaurant closes. And you can't let the dessert team work faster without touching the whole kitchen.

Microservices = a food court. Separate independent stalls — pizza, sushi, coffee — each with its own kitchen and team. The sushi stall can be upgraded, scaled, or even burn down without closing the pizza stall. But now you need shared plumbing, a directory of who's where, and stalls have to talk to each other (over the network) to serve a combined meal.

The honest tradeoff

MonolithMicroservices
DeployOne thing, simpleEach service independently
ScaleScale the whole appScale just the busy service
A crashCan take down everythingIsolated to one service
ComplexityLow — one codebaseHigh — network, discovery, monitoring
TeamsCoordinate on one repoOwn services independently
Django bridge Your Django app is almost certainly a monolith — users, orders, billing, all in one project, one deploy. And that's fine — often the right choice. Microservices solve organizational and extreme-scale problems (many teams stepping on each other, one feature needing to scale 100× more than the rest), not "my app feels big." Splitting a healthy monolith too early just trades simple problems for distributed-systems problems (network failures, the CAP tradeoffs from Week 1, harder debugging).
The interview-winning nuance "Start with a monolith; extract microservices when a specific pain forces it." The pains that justify it: independent team ownership, one component needing very different scaling, or independent deploy cadence. Saying "microservices aren't automatically better — they trade simplicity for independence" shows real judgment. Reaching for microservices by default is a classic over-engineering tell.

One supporting piece: when services are split up, how does one find another's address? Service discovery (tools like Consul) — a live directory of "which service is at which address," with health checks. It's the food court's "you are here" map.

Quick check: name one real benefit of microservices and one real cost.
Benefit (any): independent deploys, isolated failures, scale one service alone, teams own services. Cost (any): network complexity, harder debugging, service discovery/monitoring overhead, distributed-systems problems (CAP).
