The Shared Responsibility Model: one line that answers many questions

This will definitely appear on your exam — more than once. The good news: you don't memorise it. There's a single question you ask each time, and the answer falls out.

~8 min One concept: who fixes what Win: a guaranteed-marks question
If you remember one thing AWS is responsible for the security OF the cloud. You are responsible for security IN the cloud.

The whole model rests on two small English words — of and in.

An easier test: AWS handles everything you cannot touch. The data centre door, the physical server, the power, the network cabling — you can't go there, so it's AWS's job. Anything you can change from the console or an API is yours.

The story: a rented flat

You rent a flat. The landlord (AWS) is responsible for the building — the foundation, the roof, the main door, the electrical wiring, the plumbing. If the roof leaks, that's the landlord's job. You can't fix the roof; you're not even allowed to.

You (the customer) are responsible for what you put inside and what you do. Locking your own door. Who you give your key to. Your belongings. If you leave the door open and get burgled — that isn't the landlord's fault. They provided the lock; using it was your job.

AWS is exactly this. Leaving an S3 bucket public = leaving the door open. AWS gave you the lock (buckets are private by default, encryption exists, IAM exists). If you made it public yourself, that's on you, not AWS. The exam asks this shape of question repeatedly.

Both sides of the line — this table is the exam
  • ⭐ AWS's job — "security OF the cloud": data centres and their physical security, hardware and servers, power and cooling, the host operating system, the virtualization layer, the network infrastructure of Regions and AZs, and the internal maintenance of managed services (patching RDS's OS, running DynamoDB's servers).
  • ⭐ Your job — "security IN the cloud": your data (always, no exceptions), IAM users/roles/permissions, guest OS patching (on EC2), security groups and firewall rules, turning encryption on, network traffic protection, your application code.
  • ⭐ Data is ALWAYS yours. Whatever the service, however managed — classifying your data, turning on its encryption, and controlling access to it is always the customer's job. If the question is about data, the answer is almost always "customer".
  • Some things are shared ("shared controls"): patch management (AWS patches the infrastructure, you patch your guest OS and app), configuration management (AWS configures its devices, you configure your resources), and awareness/training (each trains their own people).

The part people get wrong: the line moves with the service

This is the most important subtlety, and where most marks are lost. The line doesn't stand still — the more managed the service, the more AWS takes on and the less you do.

ServiceAWS doesYou do
EC2
most work is yours		 Physical server, hypervisor, network, power. ⚠️ Patching the guest OS, installing software, security groups, firewall, encrypting data. On EC2, OS patching is yours — a favourite exam question.
RDS
managed		 Everything EC2 does, plus patching the database OS and engine, backups, replication. Users and permissions inside the database, your data, enabling encryption, which security group gets access.
S3 / Lambda / DynamoDB
most work is AWS's		 The entire infrastructure, OS, scaling, availability — all AWS. Only: who has access (IAM / bucket policy), whether encryption is on, and the data itself. Just those three.

The memory hook: on EC2 the OS is yours; on RDS the OS is AWS's. Hold that one pair and most questions solve themselves.

Exam English — the words that appear in these questions "responsible for" = whose job it is. The question really means: "who does this work?"
"patching" = applying security updates to software.
"guest OS" = the operating system running inside your EC2 instance (Ubuntu, Amazon Linux). "host OS" = the one on the real physical machine — that's AWS's.
"underlying" = the layer beneath. "Underlying infrastructure" always means AWS's side.
"on-premises" = in your own data centre, not in the cloud.
"compliance" = meeting legal or industry rules.

Bridge to your Django work You already feel this line daily. When you deploy a Django app to a managed platform, you never wonder who updates the server's Linux kernel — that's the platform's job. But whether DEBUG=False is set in your settings.py, whether ALLOWED_HOSTS is right, where your database password lives — no platform fixes those for you. That is exactly "security IN the cloud".

And the S3 question is the same thing as leaving DEBUG=True on in production. The framework gave you a safe default; you changed it. Not the framework's fault.
From memory, without scrolling up: is AWS security OF or IN the cloud? Who patches the OS on EC2? On RDS? And whose is the data — always?
AWS = security OF the cloud (you = IN). EC2 guest OS = yours. RDS OS and engine = AWS's. Data is always the customer's — no exceptions. If you got all four, these questions are free marks for you.
