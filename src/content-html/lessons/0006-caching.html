Caching: keep the hot stuff close

The cheapest way to make a system faster — and the classic tradeoff you already met in consistency.

From memory: a load balancer lets you add more servers (horizontal scaling). What's the one rule those servers must follow, and why?
They must be stateless — keep no private memory of a user — because the balancer may route your next request to a different server. Shared state (sessions) lives in a database or shared cache all servers can reach.

If you remember one thing A cache is a small, fast store of copies of data you use a lot, kept close so you don't re-do expensive work. Faster — but the copy can go stale (the tradeoff from consistency, 2a).

The story: notes on your desk vs the archive room

You work at a desk. The archive room down the hall has every document (that's your database) — complete, but a slow walk each time. So for the handful of documents you keep grabbing, you make copies and stack them on your desk. That stack is a cache: tiny, incomplete, but instant.

The trade is obvious the moment you say it: the desk copy is fast, but if the real document in the archive changes, your desk copy is now stale until you refresh it. That single tension — speed vs. staleness — is the whole story of caching.

Two words you'll hear constantly:

  • Cache hit — the copy was on your desk. Instant. 🎉
  • Cache miss — not on the desk; you walk to the archive, then (usually) drop a copy on the desk for next time.
Quick check: what's the single biggest downside of a cache?
The cached copy can be stale — out of date versus the real data in the database. Keeping them in sync ("cache invalidation") is famously the hard part.

Where caches live (just the map)

Caches sit at many points between the user and your database. You don't need to memorize these — just recognize the pattern is "keep a copy closer":

  • Browser — the user's own machine remembers images/files.
  • CDN — copies of static files (images, CSS) spread around the world, near users.
  • Application cache — an in-memory store like Redis or Memcached your app checks before the database. (This is the one interviews usually mean.)

The real skill: when do you update the cache?

This is where the senior thinking lives. There are a few named strategies; you only need the two most common to sound sharp.

1 · Cache-aside (the default)

Story: you check your desk. Not there? Walk to the archive, grab it, drop a copy on your desk, then use it. The app manages the desk.

Simple and the most common. Downside: the first request for anything is always a slow "miss" (three trips — check desk, walk to archive, copy back), and copies can drift stale.

2 · Write-through (never stale)

Story: whenever you file a new document into the archive, you simultaneously update your desk copy — same moment, every time. So the desk is never out of date.

Upside: the cache is never stale. Downside: every write is a bit slower (you do two things at once), and a fresh cache starts empty.

Two more you'll just recognize Write-behind — update the desk now, file to the archive later (fast writes, but risk losing data if the desk is wiped before filing). Refresh-ahead — predict what you'll need and refresh the desk copy just before it expires. Know the names; that's enough for now.
Django bridge Django's cache framework is cache-aside by hand: cache.get(key) → on a miss, hit the ORM, then cache.set(key, value, timeout). That timeout (TTL) is you saying "let this desk copy go stale after N seconds, then refetch." You've likely wrapped an expensive queryset in exactly this. The staleness you accept via timeout is the consistency tradeoff from 2a, made concrete.
The famous hard part "There are only two hard things in computer science: cache invalidation and naming things." Invalidation = knowing when a cached copy is out of date and refreshing it. There's no free answer — every strategy above is a different bet on how you handle it. Naming that tradeoff out loud is, again, the senior signal.
You wrap a slow report query in Django's cache with a 60-second timeout. A colleague updates the underlying data; the report shows the old numbers for up to a minute. Is this a bug?
You need a cache that is never stale, and you're OK with slightly slower writes. Which strategy?
Your win today A cache is copies of hot data kept close for speed — desk vs archive. Cache-aside (app fills it lazily) is the default; write-through (update cache + DB together) trades write speed for never being stale. The eternal cost is staleness / invalidation — the same speed-vs-freshness tradeoff you first met in consistency. That thread ties half of system design together.
